In March, a contract on Kalshi asking whether Ayatollah Ali Khamenei would be "out as Supreme Leader" had drawn $54 million in trading before the exchange paused it. That number sat in the first press release for a bill that, eight months later, has cleared the U.S. Senate without a single recorded vote against it.

The bill is S. 4035, the Discouraging Exploitative Assassination, Tragedy, and Harm Betting in Event Trading Systems Act. The short name spells DEATH BETS. The Senate passed it by voice vote on the evening of Thursday, Nov. 19, according to Cointelegraph and FinanceFeeds, which cited the Senate press gallery log. A voice vote means no roll call, so there is no tally to read. Before that, senators agreed by unanimous consent to pull the bill out of the Agriculture Committee and to adopt an amendment that narrowed it.

Sen. Adam Schiff, a California Democrat, introduced the bill on March 10. Sen. John Curtis, a Utah Republican, later signed on. After the vote Schiff wrote on X: "Congress is making it clear that these bets should be illegal."

## A narrower bill than the one filed in March

The text Schiff filed in March was broad. It would have added a new subsection to Section 5c of the Commodity Exchange Act telling any CFTC-registered exchange or clearinghouse that it may not list or clear an event contract that "involves, relates to, or references" terrorism, assassination or war. A second clause covered any contract tied to a person's death, or one that could be read as closely tracking a death.

The version that passed is tighter. Decrypt and CoinPaprika both report that the floor amendment limits the ban to contracts that expressly name terrorism, assassination, war or U.S. military operations. The death clause now covers contracts that, when listed, are reasonably expected to settle mainly on someone dying.

Sports stayed out. Schiff and Curtis filed a separate bill in March aimed at sports-style and casino-style contracts, and that one has not moved. FinanceFeeds reports that the amendment also says the bill does not touch pending court fights over sports contracts, which is where most of the prediction-market money and litigation sits.

The bill creates no new penalties. CoinDesk and CoinPaprika both note that enforcement would run through the CFTC's existing powers.

## What the CFTC can already do

Washington is not starting from zero. The Commodity Exchange Act already lets the CFTC block event contracts involving terrorism, assassination, war, gaming or illegal activity if it decides they are against the public interest. The agency's Rule 40.11 puts that into practice.

The key word is "lets." Today the call belongs to the commission. In June the CFTC proposed rewriting how it makes those public-interest calls, with factors for each category and a written process. That proposal was published in the Federal Register on June 12, drew 1,459 comments before the window closed on July 27, and has not been finalized.

Schiff's bill takes part of that discretion away. On death contracts, the ban would be flat: no exchange could list them, whatever a future commission thinks. The press releases from Schiff and Rep. Mike Levin go further and say the CFTC would lose its discretion entirely. The introduced text does not quite say that. Its war-and-terrorism clause still carried the words "as determined by the Commission." Whether the floor amendment kept that phrase could not be confirmed from the coverage reviewed for this article.

One limit is built in. The ban applies only to exchanges and clearinghouses registered with the CFTC. Offshore venues that do not hold a U.S. registration are outside its reach.

## The trades that made the case

The bill grew out of a run of markets that drew public anger this year. Analytics firm Bubblemaps found that six Polymarket wallets, all newly created, made about $1.2 million in February by betting on the timing of U.S. strikes on Iran, according to CoinDesk and CoinPaprika; Cointelegraph puts the figure at about $1 million. Nobody has identified who controlled the wallets. Polymarket also took down a market on a nuclear detonation after a backlash, The Block and Decrypt reported.

For a sense of scale, $1.2 million is small next to the $54 million that traded on the Khamenei contract, which both Schiff's and Levin's March releases cite. The issue for lawmakers is less the size of the pots than who might be on the other side. Schiff has framed the risk as insider trading: a market on when a strike lands rewards whoever learns the date first.

Neither Kalshi nor Polymarket commented on the vote in the coverage reviewed for this article.

## Why the timing is awkward for prediction markets

The vote lands in a crowded month for prediction markets in court. The Block reports that more than a dozen states have moved against Kalshi, calling its sports contracts unlicensed gambling. The CFTC under Chairman Michael Selig has answered by suing states itself; Decrypt counts 12 such suits, the latest against Louisiana. Selig said last Thursday that the agency would work with the Justice Department to defend its jurisdiction, The Block reported.

That fight is about who regulates prediction markets. The DEATH BETS Act is about what they may list. It would leave federal jurisdiction in place and fence off one category of contract. Nothing in it decides the sports fight.

## Six weeks and a committee

The House version is H.R. 7942, introduced by Levin, a California Democrat, on March 17 and sent to the House Agriculture Committee. It has not had a markup. FinanceFeeds reports that House leaders have not said whether they will schedule a vote.

The clock is short. The 119th Congress ends on Jan. 3, 2027, and any bill that has not passed both chambers by then dies. If the House wants to act, it can take up the Senate-passed S. 4035 directly rather than start its own committee process, but that still needs time on the House floor before the session ends.

Prediction markets have a nearer test first. At 11:30 a.m. Eastern time today, Justice Melissa Crane of New York County Supreme Court hears the state attorney general's request for a restraining order against Kalshi.
