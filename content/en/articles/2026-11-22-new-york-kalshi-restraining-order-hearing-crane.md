At 11:30 a.m. on Monday, in a Manhattan courtroom, a state judge will decide whether to hear the argument that could matter most to Kalshi this year. Justice Melissa Crane of New York County Supreme Court has before her Attorney General Letitia James's request for a temporary restraining order against the prediction market. Kalshi's lawyers say the order, as written, would close its exchange for every customer in the country. The attorney general's office says it would do nothing of the sort.

Two federal judges in Manhattan cleared the way for the hearing late last week. U.S. District Judge Vernon Broderick sent James's enforcement case back to state court, rejecting Kalshi's attempt to keep it in federal court. Then, on Friday evening, U.S. District Judge Lorna Schofield turned down a renewed emergency request from the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, Kalshi's federal regulator, to halt the state case. She ruled from the bench after a telephone hearing, according to The Block and ABC News.

A note on the name: in New York, the Supreme Court is the main trial court, not the top appeals court. Crane's ruling can be appealed within the state system.

## The order Kalshi says reaches everywhere

James and Gov. Kathy Hochul sued KalshiEX LLC on July 31, calling its event contracts an illegal gambling operation run without a state license. The complaint asks for forfeiture of what it calls illegal gains, restitution to users and fines equal to three times those gains. The CFTC later put the state's demand at more than $36 billion; The Block described the total as at least that much. Either way it is a claim, not a judgment, and Monday's hearing is not about money.

The restraining order is narrower in time but wider in effect. According to the proposed order described by The Block and Blockonomi, it would bar Kalshi from running an unlicensed gambling business inside New York, from New York, or for people in the state. It would cover contracts on sports, culture, elections and other events. It would also stop users under 21 from trading, since New York sets 21 as the minimum age for mobile sports betting, and ban Kalshi advertising on college campuses.

The fight is over the clause that covers business run from New York. Kalshi is headquartered in the state. In a letter to Crane on Thursday night, its lawyer Sean Murphy of Milbank argued that an order covering activity run from New York would in practice cover the whole exchange, and would likely force open positions to be closed out. He also said it would collide with an emergency order the CFTC issued on Aug. 11 telling Kalshi to keep operating.

James's office answered on Friday. It said it is not trying to shut down a federally regulated exchange nationwide, and that Kalshi would remain free to offer lawful futures and swaps. It asked Crane to give the CFTC's August order no weight.

## Washington's emergency lever

That August order is the most unusual piece of the case. The day after New York sued, Kalshi told the CFTC it faced a market emergency if a restraining order were granted. On Aug. 11 the agency used its emergency authority to direct the exchange to keep running under the Commodity Exchange Act's core principles, the basic operating rules every registered exchange must follow. "New York has no business regulating these interstate financial markets," CFTC Chairman Michael Selig said in the agency's release.

The federal government had already gone on the attack. The CFTC and the Justice Department sued New York in April, and the CFTC has been waiting since May for a ruling on its request for a preliminary injunction in that case. The agency says it has sued nine states in all, from Arizona to Wisconsin.

In court, the emergency order has not carried the weight Washington hoped. In the first days of the case, U.S. District Judge Jed Rakoff denied the CFTC's first emergency request to pause New York's suit, finding it had not shown a strong chance of winning or irreparable harm, Cointelegraph and ForkLog reported. Schofield's ruling on Friday was the second loss on the same question. In the hearing, CFTC lawyer Andrew Weisberg told her the state's order would shut Kalshi down nationwide; a lawyer for the state replied that the agency had to put in evidence of harm, according to ABC News.

The sharpest criticism came from a third judge in a different case. On Nov. 10, U.S. District Judge Colleen McMahon refused to block New York from enforcing its gambling law against Novig, a smaller prediction market. In a 35-page opinion she said Novig's sports contracts were sports betting, and wrote that the CFTC's Kalshi order looked like a regulator and a company working together to manufacture a conflict with state law, according to Bitcoin.com News and Unchained. She also called two CFTC rulemakings from Oct. 19, one treating event contracts as swaps and one saying sportsbook bets are not swaps, contradictory. Novig appealed to the Second Circuit within hours. New York's letter to Crane leans on that opinion.

Kalshi's own record in New York's federal courts is no better. On July 7 a federal judge denied its request to stop the state gaming commission from enforcing state law, a ruling Kalshi has appealed.

## Four appeals courts, two answers

Above all of this sits a legal question the federal appeals courts have split on: whether the Commodity Exchange Act, which gives the CFTC exclusive authority over exchange-traded swaps, pushes state gambling law out of the way.

The Third Circuit sided with Kalshi in April in its dispute with New Jersey. The Ninth Circuit went the other way in August and the Sixth followed in September. Last week the Fourth Circuit joined the states, ruling 2-1 that Maryland can pursue Kalshi for offering sports contracts without a license, Reuters and Unchained reported.

Even that panel could not agree on why. Judge Roger Gregory declined to decide whether Kalshi's contracts are swaps, saying gambling is a traditional state matter that Congress did not clearly take away. Judge Stephanie Thacker said the contracts are not swaps at all. Judge DeAndrea Gist Benjamin dissented, finding they are swaps under the CFTC's exclusive control. Kalshi said the split reasoning showed there was no coherent legal case against prediction markets.

So the score among federal appeals courts is three to one against Kalshi. New York sits in the Second Circuit, which has not ruled.

Crane does not have to resolve any of that on Monday. A temporary restraining order is a short-term measure meant to hold things in place until a fuller hearing. The questions in front of her are smaller and more practical: whether the state has shown enough risk of harm to act now, and whether the from-New-York wording means what Kalshi fears.

If she grants the order as drafted, Kalshi's lawyers say open positions would likely have to be unwound, including those of customers who have never set foot in the state. If she narrows it to New York residents, the company faces the kind of state-by-state limits it has fought for two years. The hearing is set for 11:30 a.m. Eastern time, and Schofield's ruling on the CFTC's broader injunction request is still pending.
