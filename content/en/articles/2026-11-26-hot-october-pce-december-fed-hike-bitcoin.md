Bitcoin fell to about $57,300 on Wednesday, a little over an hour after Washington published the inflation number the Federal Reserve watches most closely. It was back above $60,000 by the evening. The number did not go away.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis said core PCE prices, which strip out food and energy, rose 3.3% in the year to October. Economists had expected 3.2%, and September's reading was 3.1%. Several market reports put it at the highest level since late 2023. Headline PCE inflation was 3.8%, in line with forecasts, and both measures rose 0.3% on the month.

The same morning brought a revision that made the inflation figure harder to explain away. The government now says the economy grew at a 4.3% annual rate in the third quarter, up from 3.4% in its first estimate and above the roughly 3.5% forecasters expected. Most of the upgrade came from business spending on software and other intellectual property.

Put the two together and the case for a pause gets thin. Prices are rising faster than the Fed wants while the economy is running hot, not cooling.

## Two weeks, one meeting, about 94% odds

The Fed's next rate decision is on Dec. 9, the second day of a two-day meeting. Its target range sits at 3.75% to 4%. It got there in September, when it raised rates by a quarter point for the first time since July 2023. In October it held, but two officials, Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, voted for another hike.

After Wednesday's data, CME FedWatch put the chance of a December increase at about 94%, and prediction market Kalshi showed roughly 93%. Pricing had wobbled earlier in the month, so the PCE report did much of the work of firming it up.

A quarter-point move would take the range to 4% to 4.25%. That is the base case at Goldman Sachs and Bank of America, according to research notes cited in market coverage. Deutsche Bank expects three straight hikes in December, January and March, which would lift the top of the range to 4.75%.

For anyone outside bond markets, a quarter point is easy to shrug off. It is not trivial. On a $1 million floating-rate loan it adds $2,500 a year in interest. For a leveraged trader paying funding costs on a crypto position, the effect shows up faster and compounds.

## The 0.3% line Waller drew

The awkward detail for the Fed is that it set its own trigger. Governor Christopher Waller said in London on Nov. 10 that he would back at least a quarter-point increase in December. He added that a bigger move should be considered if core consumer prices rose about 0.3% or more in October.

Hours later the Labor Department reported exactly that: core CPI up 0.3% on the month against a 0.2% forecast. CME data then showed the odds of a half-point move jumping from near zero to about 15%.

Not everyone reads the data the same way. The argument for a measured response rests on what the October numbers do not show. Spending growth was soft, with nominal outlays up 0.3% and real consumption roughly flat. The saving rate rose to 4.1%. Households that save more and spend less are not usually the ones pushing prices higher.

The hawks answer with the monthly run rate. Core CPI has risen around 0.3% for three months in a row. Annualise that and it is well above the Fed's 2% target, regardless of how cautious shoppers feel.

There is also a timing problem. The November CPI report lands on Dec. 10, the day after the meeting. So the Fed will decide with October's numbers as its last full inflation reading, plus the November jobs report due on Dec. 4.

## Why bitcoin traders care about a quarter point

Bitcoin does not pay interest, so its appeal against cash and Treasury bills weakens when rates rise. That relationship is loose day to day, but it has been visible this month.

US spot bitcoin ETFs lost more than $4.9 billion in November through Tuesday, the largest monthly outflow since the funds launched in January 2024, according to fund-flow trackers. Wednesday added another $453.1 million, the seventh straight day of withdrawals. That is more than double the $2.1 billion tallied by the middle of the month.

The Crypto Fear & Greed Index, a sentiment gauge built from volatility, volume and social data, sat at 9 to 11 out of 100 this week, deep in what its makers call extreme fear. Bitcoin traded at about $60,000 on Thursday, little changed through the US holiday.

Some crypto investors argue a hike has already been absorbed, since the market priced it for weeks before the PCE report. Others point out that a second hike makes a third more likely, and that is the part futures are less sure about. Market pricing for a further hike in 2027 slipped after the data, according to reports, even as December became close to a lock.

Neither camp can settle the argument this week. US equity and bond markets are shut on Thursday for Thanksgiving and close early on Friday, so there is no ETF flow data until Monday.

## Who else is moving

The Fed is not alone in leaning hawkish. On Thursday Bank of Japan board member Naoki Tamura said Japan's 1.25% policy rate should reach a neutral level soon. That matters for crypto because cheap yen funding has long fed leveraged trades in global markets, and a stronger yen can force some of those positions to close.

Europe is moving more slowly. Euro-area money supply data on Thursday showed M3 growth steady at 3.6% in October, with lending to companies picking up to 4.5%.

The next hard data point for the Fed is the November payrolls report on Friday, Dec. 4. Five days later, at 2 p.m. in Washington, the committee publishes its decision.
