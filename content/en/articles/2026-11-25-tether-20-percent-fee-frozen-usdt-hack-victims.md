---

In 2020 a Singapore crypto exchange called Bittok lost about $50 million to hackers. Tether froze part of the stolen USDT soon after. More than five years later, the money has still not come back, and the company that bought Bittok was told by a lawyer for Tether that getting it back would cost 20%.

That detail comes from emails reviewed by Bloomberg for an investigation published on Nov. 24, as summarised by Ethnews, Startup Fortune and Phemex News. The same reporting says other theft victims recovered funds in instalments under a similar fee arrangement. None of the reports shows that any victim actually paid the 20%, and the full terms offered in each case have not been disclosed.

A Tether spokesperson told Bloomberg the company has reissued about $1.2 billion of USDT so far and that the fees pay for the legal and administrative work behind each reissuance. Chief executive Paolo Ardoino defended returning money in stages in civil cases. His reasons, as reported: rival parties can claim the same funds, and there is no clear legal guidance on who gets what.

## A 10% policy that does not cover theft

Tether does publish a fee schedule, but for a different problem. Its token recovery policy covers users who send USDT by mistake to an unsupported address or an incompatible smart contract. For those cases it charges up to 10% of the amount recovered or $1,000, whichever is greater, and it keeps the right to refuse.

Hacks, scams and police requests sit outside that page. They go through a separate information-request process with no published price. So the 20% quoted to Bittok's owner is twice the highest rate Tether lists anywhere, yet Ethnews points out it cannot automatically be called a breach of policy, because no written policy applies to theft.

The practical result for a victim is uncertainty. Nobody outside Tether can tell whether fees are set the same way from case to case, or whether a claimant can challenge one.

## How a frozen token becomes a new one

The mechanics explain why victims depend on Tether's goodwill. USDT on Ethereum has two administrative functions that matter here. One blacklists an address, which stops it sending tokens. The other destroys the balance held by a blacklisted address. Tether can then mint the same amount of fresh USDT and send it to whoever it decides is the rightful owner.

In plain terms, the stolen coins are never handed back. They are deleted, and new ones are printed. Only the issuer can do both steps, which is why the issuer, not a court or the victim, controls the timing and, as Bloomberg's reporting suggests, the price.

The $1.2 billion reissued so far also hides a lot. On-chain records show tokens being destroyed and new ones minted, but not who finally received them, so outsiders cannot tell how much of that total went to hack victims rather than to law enforcement.

## The Binance test case

The story lands four weeks after hackers took more than $6 billion from Binance on Oct. 29. The FBI attributed the attack to the North Korean group it calls TraderTraitor. Ardoino said on Nov. 2: "Within minutes of the hack, our team had frozen more than $1.5 billion." An on-chain review by Bitquery, cited by Bitcoin.com News, timed Tether's first freeze at 37 minutes after the main Ethereum drain, and about $729 million of stolen USDT moved out before the freezes took hold.

So the $1.5 billion locked by Tether after Binance is larger than everything it says it has reissued in its history. A Tether spokesperson told Reuters the company planned to work with Binance and the authorities on reissuing those tokens. Whether a fee will be attached, and at what rate, has not been made public.

U.S. prosecutors are also involved. On Nov. 12 the Justice Department won court approval to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the stolen funds, part of more than $2.7 billion frozen by authorities and private companies, The Block reported.

## Circle waits for a judge

Tether's main rival handles this differently, and it has drawn its own criticism. Circle, which issues USDC, waits for court orders before moving frozen funds, according to Bloomberg, a practice that has cost some victims time. Together the two companies issue about 86% of all stablecoins.

Circle's position is that it cannot simply burn and replace tokens sitting in a wallet it does not control. In a July court filing in Wisconsin, it argued it had no technical or legal way to destroy or reissue USDC held in a third-party wallet, AMBCrypto reported. It has been working with the Justice Department on a route where it voluntarily issues replacement USDC after a final forfeiture order. That route is now being used for the Binance funds: Circle has said it will mint replacement USDC for the U.S. government once a judge orders forfeiture.

The trade-off is plain. Tether can move fast and decide for itself, but it sets its own terms. Circle hands the decision to a court, which takes longer but leaves a public record.

## Where regulation stops

The GENIUS Act, the U.S. stablecoin law signed in July 2025, requires permitted issuers to be able to freeze, seize and burn tokens and to comply with lawful orders. It says nothing about what an issuer may charge a theft victim to get money back.

For Bittok's owner, five years on, the question is still unanswered. For Binance, it is worth $1.5 billion. Tether has not said when it will reissue the frozen Binance tokens or on what terms, and the Justice Department's forfeiture case for the USDC portion still needs a judge's order before Circle mints anything.
