A U.S. derivatives exchange that keeps $100 million of bitcoin on its balance sheet can now tell its regulator it has up to $80 million toward its rainy-day fund. Until Monday, staff had not put in writing how much, if anything, that bitcoin was worth for this purpose.

Staff at the Commodity Futures Trading Commission's Division of Market Oversight and Division of Clearing and Risk issued the advisory on Monday, Nov. 23. It covers the three kinds of trading infrastructure the agency registers: designated contract markets (futures exchanges), swap execution facilities and derivatives clearing organizations, the clearinghouses that stand between buyers and sellers. Cointelegraph, FinanceFeeds and investingLive all reported the same core terms.

Each of those entities has to show the CFTC that it holds enough financial resources to cover at least 12 months of operating costs on a rolling basis. The rule exists so that a venue with open positions does not collapse just because it ran out of money to pay staff and servers. The question the advisory answers is narrow: can crypto sitting on the company's own books count toward that year of runway?

## Eighty cents on the dollar

Yes, with a discount. Bitcoin and ether can be counted after a valuation haircut of at least 20%. Stablecoins that qualify as payment stablecoins take a haircut of at least 2%, so $100 million of them would count as up to $98 million. Other crypto assets that are not readily marketable and are not payment stablecoins would generally take a 100% haircut, which means they count for nothing.

A haircut is simply the share of an asset's market value a regulator ignores, to allow for the price falling before the asset can be sold. Last week shows why 20% is not a generous number. Bitcoin traded near $65,100 on Nov. 17 and dropped below $56,000 on Nov. 21, according to price data cited by CryptoTicker. That is a fall of roughly 14% in four days, most of the cushion gone in a single week. On Tuesday morning in Europe it was back near $60,150.

The firms most likely to use the room are the crypto-native venues already under the CFTC. Cointelegraph lists Coinbase Derivatives, Crypto.com's North American Derivatives Exchange, Bitnomial Exchange, and the prediction markets Kalshi and Polymarket US among CFTC-regulated entities. For any of them holding crypto on the balance sheet, the advisory now spells out what it is worth on paper.

## The cash floor stays

The advisory leaves one requirement untouched, and it is the one that matters on a bad day. On top of the 12-month total, each entity must hold a separate liquidity buffer in unencumbered cash or highly liquid securities. For swap execution facilities that buffer covers three months of operating costs. For futures exchanges and clearinghouses it covers six months, according to FinanceFeeds and investingLive. Crypto cannot be used to meet it, because bitcoin is neither cash nor a highly liquid security in accounting terms.

Put the two rules together and the ceiling becomes clear. A futures exchange or clearinghouse must still fund half of its year of runway in cash or Treasuries. Crypto can only help fill the other half, and only at 80 cents per dollar at best.

Staff also told firms to think about wrong-way risk. That is the chance that a business loses money at the same moment its crypto loses value. A crypto derivatives exchange is the textbook case: when prices crash, trading can dry up, fees fall and the bitcoin on the balance sheet shrinks all at once. The advisory asks firms to weigh that link rather than treat the haircut as the end of the analysis. InvestingLive adds that clearinghouses are expected to build liquidity risk into the haircuts they choose and review them each month.

## Where it stops

Two limits shape how far this goes. The first is that the advisory says nothing about default resources. Those are the pools a clearinghouse draws on when a member fails and cannot pay its losses. Operating money and default money are different buckets, and only the first one is affected here.

The second is legal status. This is staff guidance from two divisions, not a rule voted on by the Commission. FinanceFeeds notes it does not create enforceable rights and is not a no-action position. Staff can revise it, and a future Commission is not bound by it.

## Built on March's broker rules

The numbers are not new. On March 20, the CFTC's Market Participants Division and the Division of Clearing and Risk published a set of frequently asked questions for futures brokers, known as futures commission merchants. That guidance told brokers holding bitcoin or ether for their own account to apply a minimum 20% capital charge, and a 2% charge for payment stablecoins, according to The Block and BeInCrypto. Both outlets said the figures matched guidance the SEC's Division of Trading and Markets had given broker-dealers.

That March document followed a pilot announced on Dec. 8, 2025, by then-acting Chair Caroline Pham, which let futures brokers accept bitcoin, ether and the USDC stablecoin as customer collateral, as The Block reported at the time. The agency, now led by Chairman Michael Selig, has since moved step by step from customer collateral to broker capital and now to the venues themselves.

Seen that way, Monday's advisory closes a gap more than it opens a door. A futures broker could already count bitcoin at 80% under its capital rules. The exchange that broker trades on could not say the same about its own operating reserves. Now the two sides of the market use the same discount.

## What the haircut cannot measure

There is one thing the 20% figure does not capture. It assumes the bitcoin can be sold at roughly the screen price. In a week like the one just past, when U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs reported a net outflow of about $570 million on Monday alone, according to SoSoValue data cited by CryptoTicker and CoinGape, the sale itself can move the price. That is exactly the scenario the cash floor exists for.

For now the advisory changes balance sheets, not trading. The next test will come when these exchanges file their next financial resources reports with the CFTC and show how much of their runway they have chosen to hold in crypto.
